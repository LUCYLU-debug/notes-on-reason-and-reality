    Part VI / Freedom & Meaning · Chapter 08

    Compounding, Risk & Financial Freedom

    The appeal of financial independence is not necessarily luxury. It can be the possibility of choosing work for reasons other than the need to pay next month's bills. How does a modest beginning become a durable source of choice?
    Compound returnsRisk and opportunity costFinancial independence

    1. What actually compounds?

    For an initial amount \(P\), a constant per-period return \(r\), and \(n\) periods of reinvestment, the familiar formula is

    \[ A_n=P(1+r)^n. \]

    The formula describes reinvestment under an assumed constant rate. It is not a promise that any real asset will grow smoothly. Deposits may pay a stated rate for a period; market investments have changing, sometimes negative returns. Taxes, fees, inflation and losses change the outcome.

    There is a useful distinction between nominal wealth and purchasing power. If prices rise, the same dollar amount buys less. Likewise, two investments with similar gross returns can deliver different net returns because of fees and tax treatment. New Zealand's Financial Markets Authority emphasises the relationship between objectives, time horizon, risk and diversification.1

    The most important consequence of compounding is not that a small initial sum must inevitably become large. It is that the rate retained after costs, the period of reinvestment, additional contributions and the avoidance of irreversible losses all matter.

    2. What do Buffett and Munger actually contribute?

    Warren Buffett's 2013 Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letter argued that many non-professional investors are better served by broad, low-cost index exposure than by paying substantial active-management fees. This is an argument about costs, diversification and the difficulty of outperforming markets, not a guarantee of high returns or a universal asset-allocation prescription.2

    Charlie Munger's wider contribution was a method of judgement: drawing on ideas from more than one discipline, examining incentives, and avoiding errors that arise when a person relies on a single familiar mental model. Poor Charlie's Almanack collects this approach.3

    These ideas belong together. The mathematics of compounding describes how returns accumulate; disciplined reasoning concerns how to avoid undermining that process. There is no contradiction between understanding investment principles and deciding that a given investment is unsuitable.

    3. How should someone think about money when income is low?

    At the beginning of accumulation, the size of the initial portfolio may matter less than avoiding expensive mistakes and developing future options.

    A useful order of priorities is to secure essential spending, understand high-cost debt, establish readily accessible emergency savings, and then choose investments appropriate to the time horizon and risks. New Zealand's independent Sorted guidance suggests beginning with a small emergency fund and building towards several months of expenses; it advises keeping emergency money out of volatile investments.4

    Income-generating capability is itself valuable. Education, marketable skills, work experience and professional relationships may increase future earnings, although their returns are uncertain and uneven. For someone with very limited disposable income, a carefully chosen investment in capability can be more consequential than trying to maximise the return on a tiny portfolio.

    A small, sustainable saving habit can still have value. Its importance is partly mathematical and partly institutional: it creates a buffer and reduces the need to make hurried decisions. But a life in which every pleasurable purchase is treated as a failure has confused money's purpose with its accumulation. Spending on meaningful experiences, relationships or health can be rational, provided essential obligations and longer-term objectives remain covered.

    4. What would it mean for investment income to cover living costs?

    The goal needs a precise definition. Interest, dividends, asset appreciation and sustainable withdrawals are not interchangeable. An investment portfolio can rise in value without paying enough cash income to cover bills; a high dividend yield can accompany falling capital value; and a withdrawal plan can involve selling assets.

    Suppose annual living costs are NZ$30,000 measured in today's purchasing power. If a portfolio could reliably generate a 2% annual real return after tax and fees, and the entire return were available for spending without reducing real principal, a simplified perpetuity calculation would be

    \[ K=\frac{30{,}000}{0.02}=\text{NZ}\$1{,}500{,}000. \]

    At an assumed 3% net real return, the corresponding arithmetic is NZ$1,000,000. These are illustrative conditional calculations, not forecasts, recommended targets or assurances that such returns can be earned without risk. Real-life expenditure and returns change, tax rules differ, and maintaining capital while drawing income may be difficult.

    The frequently discussed “4% rule” is also not a guaranteed interest rate. It originated as a historical withdrawal analysis involving specified portfolio and retirement-horizon assumptions; it cannot simply be substituted for a perpetual, risk-free yield.5

    A sensible definition of financial independence therefore includes more than one number. It requires clarity about essential and discretionary expenses, liquidity, downside risk, inflation, taxation, and how much uncertainty the person can tolerate. The desired freedom is a capacity to refuse unwanted work—not merely a portfolio balance displayed on a screen.

    5. Can health compound too?

    The metaphor has limits. Regular physical activity can improve fitness and reduce important health risks, as described in World Health Organization guidelines.6 Skills and relationships can also benefit from sustained attention. Yet none follows a fixed exponential return formula; illness, ageing, chance and changing circumstances matter.

    The common principle is not exponential growth. It is that small choices can have consequences extending beyond the day on which they are made. The danger is imagining that every area of life ought to produce a measurable return. Rest, care and enjoyment need not justify themselves as investments.

    Where the argument leadsMoney is valuable as a source of security and choice. Long-term thinking is useful, but financial independence should not demand the sacrifice of everything that makes independence desirable in the first place.
